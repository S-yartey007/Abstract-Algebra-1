\textbf{Definition 1.1:} Let $G$ be a non-empty set, equipped with a binary operation, that is, a "multiplication" map % []
$$ \boldsymbol{\cdot} : G \times G \longrightarrow G$$ % []
Our notations will be % []
$$ \boldsymbol{\cdot} \, (g,h) =: g \bullet h$$ % []
or simply $gh$ if the name of the operation can be understood. % []

\textbf{Definition 1.2:} A set $G$, endowed with a binary operation $( \cdot ) \, (ie (G,\cdot)) $, is a group if: % [, 8, 9]
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[i)] the operation is associative, that is % [0, 11]
    $$(\forall g,h,k \in G) \quad (g \cdot h) \cdot k = g \cdot (h \cdot k);$$ % [2]
    \item[ii)] there exists an identity element $e_G$ for $G$, that is % [3, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18]
    $$(\exists e_G \in G)(\forall g \in G): g \cdot e_G = g = e_G \cdot g;$$ % [9]
    \item[iii)] every element in $G$ has an inverse with respect to $\cdot$, that is, % [0]
    $$(\forall g \in G)(\exists h \in G): g \cdot h = e_G = h \cdot g.$$ % [0]
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Commutative groups} % [8]

Given a group $(G, \cdot)$ such that $(\forall g, h \in G)$ $g \cdot h = h \cdot g$, we say that the operation is commutative. % [9, 40]
We say that two elements $g, h$ "commute" if $gh = hg$. % [1]
In a commutative group, any two elements commute. Such groups are said to be abelian groups. % [2]

The notation used in treating abelian groups differs from that used for standard groups. % [3]
The operation in an abelian group $A$ is, as a rule, denoted by $+$ and is called addition. % [4, 45]
The identity is called $0_A$ and the inverse of an element $a \in A$ is denoted by $-a$. % [6, 48]

Now in a standard group setting, the conventional power notation can be used $g^0 = e_G$ and for a positive integer $n$: % [7, 49, 50]
$$g^n = g \cdots g \quad \text{($n$-times)}$$ % [1, 52, 53]
$$g^{-n} = g^{-1} \cdots g^{-1} \quad \text{($n$-times)}$$ % [4]

On the other hand, in the abelian group setting the power notation is replaced by 'multiple' $0a = 0$ and for a positive integer $n$: % [5, 56, 57, 58]
$$na = a + a + \cdots + a \quad \text{($n$-times)}$$ % [9]
$$(-n)a = (-a) + \cdots + (-a) \quad \text{($n$-times)}$$ % [9]

\subsection*{Order} % [1]

\textbf{Definition 1.6:} An element $g$ of a group $G$ has finite order if $g^n = e$ for some positive integer $n$. % [2]
In this case, the order $|g|$ is \underline{the smallest positive} $n$ such that $g^n = e$. % [2, 63, 67]
If $g$ does not have finite order, then $|g| = \infty$. % [5, 69, 70]
By definition, if $g^n = e$ for some positive integer $n$, then $|g| \le n$. % [1, 72]

\textbf{Lemma 1.7:} If $g^n = e$ for some integer $n$, then $|g|$ is a divisor of $n$. % [5]

\textbf{Proof:} By definition of order, $n \ge |g|$, that is $n - |g| \ge 0$. % [6, 78, 80, 82]
Thus by the division algorithm $\exists 0 \le r < |g| , m \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that $n = |g| \cdot m +  r.$ So that $ r = n - |g|\cdot m \ge 0$. % [1, 83, 84]
Note that % [5]
$$g^r = g^{n - |g| \cdot m} = g^n \cdot (g^{|g|})^{-m} = e \cdot e^{-m} = e$$ % [6]
By definition of order, $|g|$ is the smallest positive integer such that $g^{|g|} = e$. % [7]
Since $r$ is smaller than $|g|$ and $g^r = e$, $r$ cannot be positive, hence $r = 0$ necessarily. % [8, 89, 90]
So that $0 = n - |g| \cdot m$. % [1, 92]
Thus we have $n = |g| \cdot m$, proving that $n$ is indeed an integer multiple of $|g|$ as claimed. % [3, 94, 95, 96, 97]

\textbf{Corollary 1.8:} Let $g$ be an element of finite order, and let $N \in \mathbb{Z}$. % [00, 101, 104]
Then $g^N = e \iff N \text{ is a multiple of } |g|$. % [05]

\textbf{Definition1.12:} A permutation of a set $A$ is a bijective map $A \to A$. % [07]

\textbf{Definition1.13:} Let $A$ be a set. The symmetric group or group of permutations of $A$, denoted $S_A$, is the group of all bijective maps from $A$ to itself under composition. % [08, 210]

The group of permutations of the set $\{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$ is denoted by $S_n$. % [11]
The terminology is easily justified, the bijections from A to itself amounts to permutining the elements of A. This operation may be viewed as a transformation of A wihcih does not change it(as a set) hence a "symmetry" ie groups $S_A$ are very large. For example if $A = \{1,...n\}$ tehn $|S_n| = n!$
To get a feel  for what these are we consider these examples
$S_1$ is a trivial group, $S_2$ consists of the two possible permutations
$$
\begin{cases}
1 \mapsto 1\\
2 \mapsto 2
\end{cases}
and \begin{cases}
    1 \mapsto 2 \\
    2 \mapsto 1
\end{cases}
$$
Which one call call e(identity) and f(flip) with operation
$$ee=ff=e, ef=fe=f$$

\textbf{Notation:} We will indicate an element $\sigma \in S_n$ by listing the effect of applying $\sigma$ underneath the list $1, \ldots, n$ as a matrix. % [28, 229, 230, 231, 233]
Thus the elements $e, f$ in $S_2$ may be denoted by: % [35]
$$e = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad f = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$ %

Using the same notation, $S_3$ consists of

$$ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 3 & 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 3 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} $$

$$
Continuiing in this fashion we obtain the desired results.

\subsection*{Dihedral groups}
A symmetry is a transformation which preserves a structure. The symmetries will naturally form groups. One context in which this notion may be visualised clearly is that of 'geometric figures' such as polygons in the plane.

The dihedral groups may be defined as these groups of symmetries for the regular polygons. Placing the polygon so that it is centered at the origin (thereby excluding translations as possible symmetries), we see that the dihedral group for a regular $n$-sided polygon consists of the $n$ rotations by $2\pi/n$ radians about the origin, and $n$ distinct reflections about lines through the origin and a vertex or a midpoint of a side. Thus the dihedral group for a regular $n$-sided polygon consists of $2n$ elements. We will denote this group by $D_{2n}$.
We will denote this group by $D_{2n}$
There is a way to relate dihedral groups to symmetric groups by considering the fact that a symmetry of a regular polygon is determined by what happens to its vertices.For instance, Label the vertices of an equilateral triangle by $1, 2, 3$. Then a counterclockwise rotation by an angle of $2\pi/3$ sends 1 to 3, 3 to 2, and 2 to 1 and no other symmetry of the triangle does the same.In other words with the counterclockwise rotation of the equivalent triangle by $2 \pi/3$ we associate to it the permutation.
$$ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 3 & 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \in S_3 $$
Such a labeling defines a function $D_6 \longrightarrow S_3$ which is a group homomorphism, and in addition this function is injective. We can think of this as a faithful action of $D_6$ on the set $\{1,2,3\}$.
These function induced by the labelings are group homomorphisms.

\textbf{Lemma 1.13:} If $a \equiv a' \pmod n$ and $b \equiv b' \pmod n$, then
$$ a + b \equiv a' + b' \pmod n $$

$$ (a' + b') - (a + b) = (a' - a) + (b' - b) = kn + ln = (k + l)n $$

\section*{Group Homomorphisms}

Let $(G, m_G)$ and $(H, m_H)$ be two groups where.
$$ m_G : G \times G \mapsto G \quad and \quad m_H : H \times H \mapsto H$$

\textbf{Definition 2.1:} A map $\varphi : G \mapsto H$ is a group homomorphism if $\forall a, b \in G$,
$$ \varphi(a \cdot b) = \varphi(a) \cdot \varphi(b) $$
In other words, $\varphi$ is a homomorphism if it 'preserves the structure'.

\textbf{Proposition 2.2:} Let $\varphi : G \to H$ be a group homomorphism. Then
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $\varphi(e_G) = e_H$
    \item $\forall g \in G, \varphi(g^{-1}) = \varphi(g)^{-1}$
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Proof:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item To prove the first item, we make use of the cancellation property and the definition of homomorphism. Since $e_H = e_H \cdot e_H$,
    $$ e_H  \cdot \varphi(e_G) = \varphi(e_G) = \varphi(e_G \cdot e_G) = \varphi(e_G) \cdot \varphi(e_G) $$
    which implies $e_H = \varphi(e_G)$ by cancelling $\varphi(e_G)$.
    \item $\varphi(g^{-1}) \cdot \varphi(g) = \varphi(g^{-1} \cdot g) = \varphi(e_G) = e_H = \varphi(g)^{-1} \cdot \varphi(g)$
    Thus $\varphi(g^{-1}) = \varphi(g)^{-1}$ by cancellation.
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Subgroups}

\textbf{Definition 3.1:} Let $(G, \cdot)$ be a group. A subgroup $(H, \cdot)$ of $G$ is a non-empty subset $H$ which is also a group under the binary operation of $G$.

\textbf{Proposition 3.2:} A non-empty subset $H$ of a group $G$ is a subgroup if and only if
$$ \forall (a, b \in H) : ab^{-1} \in H $$

\textbf{Lemma 3.3:} If $\{H_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in A}$ is any family of subgroups of a group $G$, then
$$ H = \bigcap_{\alpha \in A} H_\alpha $$
is a subgroup of $G$.

\textbf{Lemma 3.4:} Let $\varphi : G \to G'$ be a group homomorphism, and let $H'$ be a subgroup of $G'$. Then $\varphi^{-1}(H')$ is a subgroup of $G$.

\textbf{Definition 3.5 (Kernel and Image):}
Every group homomorphism $\varphi : G \to G'$ determines two interesting subgroups:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the kernel of $\varphi$, $\ker \varphi \subseteq G$; and
    \item the image of $\varphi$, $\text{im } \varphi \subseteq G'$.
\end{itemize}
The kernel of $\varphi$ is the subset of $G$ consisting of elements mapping to the identity in $G'$:
$$ \ker \varphi := \{ g \in G \mid \varphi(g) = e_{G'} \} = \varphi^{-1}(\{e_{G'}\}) $$

\textbf{Definition 3.6:} A group $G$ is finitely generated if there exists a finite subset $A \subseteq G$ such that $G = \langle A \rangle$.
